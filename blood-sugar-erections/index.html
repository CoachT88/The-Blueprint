Can High Blood Sugar Affect Erections?

Yes. Blood sugar that stays high over a long period can damage the blood vessels and the nerves that erections depend on, which is why erectile difficulty is one of the recognised complications of diabetes.

The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases puts it plainly: erectile dysfunction, reduced interest in sex and bladder problems can happen when diabetes damages your blood vessels and nerves. It also notes that men with diabetes may develop erectile difficulty ten to fifteen years earlier than men without it.

That is worth knowing for two reasons. The first is that this is a physical mechanism, not a matter of effort or desire. The second is better news: blood sugar is one of the few contributors to erection problems you can actually measure, with a blood test that takes a few minutes and does not require fasting.

This guide covers how the mechanism works, what to look for, what helps, and a seven-day habits check you can run on your own.

1. How an erection depends on blood flow and nerve signals

An erection is a circulation event with a nervous system switch.

Arousal travels along nerves to the blood vessels in the penis. Those vessels relax and widen, blood flows in faster than it drains out, and the tissue becomes firm. Mayo Clinic describes arousal as involving the brain, hormones, nerves, muscles and blood vessels together, which is a useful way to hold it in your head: a problem anywhere along that chain can show up as the same symptom.

Two parts of that chain are the ones high blood sugar reaches.

The vessels. They have to widen on demand. Vessels that have been damaged over years are less able to do that.

The nerves. They have to deliver the signal. The nerves involved here are largely autonomic, meaning the ones you do not consciously control, the same set that manages the bladder.

That shared wiring is why erection changes and bladder changes often turn up together, and why a clinician will usually ask about both.

2. What high blood sugar does over time

NIDDK describes the mechanism directly: over time, high blood glucose and high levels of fats such as triglycerides in the blood can damage your nerves and the small blood vessels that nourish those nerves. The result is autonomic neuropathy, which affects the nerves that serve the bladder and the sex organs.

Three features of this matter for how you think about it.

It is cumulative. The damage reflects years of exposure, not what you ate yesterday.

It is quiet. There is usually no pain and no obvious moment when it starts.

It travels with company. NIDDK lists the things that raise the risk together: blood glucose that is not well controlled, untreated high blood pressure or cholesterol, excess weight, inactivity, smoking and heavy drinking. These tend to arrive as a group rather than one at a time.

Diabetes is not the only cause of erection difficulty, and having erection difficulty does not mean you have diabetes. NIDDK's list of causes also includes heart and blood vessel disease, hormone problems, nerve damage from other conditions, several common medicines including antidepressants and blood pressure treatments, and mental or emotional factors.

3. Three principles worth keeping

These are the ideas that make the rest of the guide usable. If you remember nothing else, remember these.

An erection is a measurement of your circulation that you take several times a week

Why it matters
You are not normally given feedback on the state of your blood vessels. Nothing hurts. Nothing shows up in a mirror. Erectile function is one of the few places where circulation reports on itself regularly, which is why the American Heart Association advises raising sexual difficulty with a healthcare professional to see whether heart disease, anxiety or depression could be behind it.
Practical example
A man in his forties notices over about a year that morning erections have become less frequent and less firm. Nothing else has changed, and he feels fine. That slow drift is information, and it is the kind that is easy to explain away.
Apply it
Treat a sustained change in erection quality as a reason to book a general health check, not only a sexual health conversation. Ask for blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar to be looked at together.

Nerve damage from high blood sugar is painless, so the early signs show up somewhere other than pain

Why it matters
People expect nerve damage to hurt. Autonomic neuropathy generally does not. It shows up instead as things that work less reliably than they used to, and the bladder and the sex organs are served by the same group of nerves. NIDDK notes that damage to the bladder nerves can make it hard to tell when you need to urinate and when your bladder is empty.
Practical example
A man mentions erection difficulty to his doctor but does not mention that he has been getting up twice a night, or that he sometimes does not feel a full bladder until it is urgent. He treats them as two unrelated annoyances. They may share a cause.
Apply it
When you describe the erection problem, describe the bladder pattern in the same sentence. It changes what the clinician is looking for.

A blood test settles in a week what guessing cannot settle in a year

Why it matters
The A1C test reflects your average blood glucose over roughly the past three months. NIDDK notes it does not require fasting and blood can be drawn at any time of day. It is the difference between wondering and knowing, and the answer changes what you do next.
Practical example
Two men have identical symptoms. One spends eight months reading forums and trying supplements. The other asks his doctor for a blood panel at an appointment he was attending anyway, and gets a number.
Apply it
At your next appointment, ask whether an A1C test is appropriate for you. It is a question, not a self-diagnosis, and a clinician will tell you whether it fits your situation and risk factors.
The one to remember

Erectile function is circulation you can observe. A sustained change in it is worth a blood panel, not a supplement.

4. What to look for in your own situation

None of the following diagnoses anything. They are the details a clinician will ask about, and having thought about them first makes the appointment more useful.

  • Has the change been gradual over months or years, or did it arrive suddenly?
  • Does it happen in every situation, including alone, or mainly with a partner?
  • Have morning erections become less frequent or less firm?
  • Are you noticeably thirstier than you used to be, or urinating more often, including at night?
  • Has your weight, blood pressure or cholesterol changed, or gone unmeasured for several years?
  • Does diabetes run in your family?
  • Have you started any new medication in the period the change began?

A gradual change present in every situation, alongside increased thirst or urination, points in a different direction from a sudden change that only happens with a partner. The second pattern is covered in our guide on getting hard alone but losing it during sex.

5. What actually helps

The honest summary is that the habits which support metabolic health are the same ones that support vascular health, and there is no shortcut that skips them.

Movement, in the amount that is actually recommended. The NHS guidance for adults aged nineteen to sixty-four is at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking or cycling, spread across four or five days, plus strengthening activity that works the major muscle groups on at least two days. Muscle strengthening does not count toward the 150 minutes, so it is in addition.

Sitting less. The NHS advises adults to sit down less throughout the day, at work, travelling and at home, while noting there is not currently enough evidence to set a specific daily limit.

Sleep. Short sleep is associated with worse metabolic health, and it is usually the easiest of these to change first.

Not changing your medication on your own. Some antidepressants and blood pressure treatments can affect sexual function. The American Heart Association is explicit that you should not stop heart medication because of worries about sex drive or function. Raise it with your prescriber instead, because there are often alternatives.

Smoking and alcohol. Both appear on NIDDK's list of things that raise the risk of diabetes-related sexual problems.

None of this is a treatment for erectile dysfunction, and none of it replaces a medical assessment. It is the groundwork that makes everything else more likely to help.

6. Try This: Your 7-Day Metabolic Habits Check

Section 4 asks questions most men answer from memory. This is a way to answer them from observation.

It takes about a minute a day for seven days. You need paper or the notes app already on your phone. There is nothing to buy, nothing to sign up for, and no personal information to hand over to anyone, including us. Nobody else needs to see it. You can also print this page and write on it.

Each day, write down five things

  1. MovementRoughly how many minutes you moved enough to breathe harder, and whether any of it was strength work.
  2. SleepHours, and whether it felt restful.
  3. MealsRoughly when you ate and whether the day was mostly cooked food or mostly convenience food. No calorie counting.
  4. Thirst and urinationWhether you were thirstier than usual, and how many times you got up in the night.
  5. ErectionsMorning erection full, partial or none. If there was sexual activity, what it was like.

Keep each entry to a line. The value is in having seven days side by side, not in writing a lot on any one of them.

After seven days, read it back

Lay the week out and look for four things:

  • How many days hit something close to thirty minutes of movement, and how many days had none?
  • Do the nights with less sleep line up with the days you felt worse?
  • Did thirst or night-time urination show up on most days, some, or none?
  • Do morning erections appear on most days, some days, or none?

Then do the one thing this week is actually for: take the page to a clinician. A week of specifics turns "I think things have changed" into something a doctor can work with, and it gives you a concrete opening to ask whether an A1C test is appropriate for you.

What this check cannot do. It cannot diagnose diabetes, prediabetes or any other condition, and it is not a test. Diabetes is diagnosed with blood tests ordered and interpreted by a healthcare professional. NIDDK also notes that a result showing diabetes or prediabetes in someone without symptoms should be repeated on a different day to confirm it, which is a good illustration of how much care goes into an actual diagnosis and how little a week of notes can substitute for one.

A week that looks fine is not a reason to skip an appointment you were already considering. Thirst and frequent urination can be symptoms worth prompt medical attention, so if either is pronounced, do not wait seven days to mention it.

7. When to see a doctor

Arrange a medical evaluation if you are repeatedly having trouble getting or keeping an erection. NIDDK describes the standard assessment as a review of your medical, sexual and mental health history, a physical examination, and laboratory tests.

Make the appointment sooner if any of these apply:

  • You are noticeably thirstier than usual, urinating more often, or losing weight without trying.
  • Your morning erections have become clearly less frequent.
  • You have a family history of diabetes, or you have been told in the past that you have prediabetes.
  • Your blood pressure or cholesterol has not been checked in several years.
  • You have known cardiovascular risk factors, or you smoke.
  • The change arrived suddenly, or is getting worse.

It is reasonable to ask directly whether an A1C test is appropriate. NIDDK's reference ranges are below 5.7 per cent as normal, 5.7 to 6.4 per cent as prediabetes and 6.5 per cent or above as diabetes, with the caveat that some conditions can make the test less accurate and that a clinician interprets the result in the context of everything else. Prediabetes is not a diagnosis of diabetes, and NIDDK advises annual testing for type 2 diabetes if you have it.
