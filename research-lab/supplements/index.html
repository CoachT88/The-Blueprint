Supplement evidence

The ingredient gets the attention. The evidence gets the last word.

Men compare nitric oxide products, herbal supplements and semen-volume blends every day. Which claims have actually been tested in humans? Which are still community stories?

We separate the purpose of an ingredient, the outcome studied and what remains unanswered. This page does not recommend a supplement stack, dose or brand.

Read this before buying: Ingredients marketed for sexual enhancement are not interchangeable with tested medical treatments. Some commercial products contain undisclosed drug ingredients. A personalized medical review matters, especially if you take prescriptions.

1. Citrulline: interesting, still early

Preliminary evidence

L-citrulline is converted to arginine and is discussed for nitric oxide pathways. In a small trial involving men with mild erectile dysfunction, erectile hardness improved during the citrulline phase for some participants. The design was small and short; it cannot establish a reliable result for most men or replace proven treatment.

Community question: A man may report better fullness during training. Was there any blinded comparison, a consistent outcome measure, or enough follow-up to separate expectation and normal variation?

2. Arginine: more trials, still limitations

Supported by clinical evidence

A meta-analysis of randomized trials reported improved erectile-function outcomes in some men with erectile dysfunction. Included studies differed in formulations, exposure and duration. An average effect across trials does not predict an individual outcome, and medical treatment may be more appropriate.

3. Ginseng: a bigger evidence base does not guarantee a big benefit

Preliminary evidence

A Cochrane review found that ginseng may have little or only trivial effects on erectile function or intercourse satisfaction compared with placebo when assessed with validated questionnaires. This is a useful reminder that popular does not mean established.

4. Zinc, lecithin, pygeum and semen-volume blends

Community-reported, unverified

These names often appear in personal accounts about ejaculate volume or force. The outcomes people discuss are different: semen volume, force of ejaculation, orgasm sensation and fertility. A report about one cannot establish the others.

These community combinations have not been established as reliable methods to increase ejaculate volume in otherwise healthy men. Nutrient deficiencies deserve appropriate assessment; routine high-dose supplementation can create risks. A meta-analysis of abstinence studies also found that the time since previous ejaculation influences semen volume, a major confounder in supplement journals.

5. The safety problem with branded sexual enhancers

NCCIH warns that products marketed for sexual enhancement have been found with unlisted drug ingredients. A specific FDA warning issued in October 2026 documented hidden sildenafil in a product marketed for sexual enhancement. That matters for people taking nitrate medicines and those with cardiovascular risk.

Do not combine supplements or change medication based on forum experiments. Ask a pharmacist or clinician to check interactions and medical conditions before taking a new supplement.

6. What to examine before believing a report

Ask what ingredient was taken, what actually changed, how the outcome was measured, whether other changes happened at the same time, and whether an independent study supports the same claim. The answers are more useful than a dramatic testimonial.
