The Research Lab

Manual Techniques

Four practices are covered here: directional pulls, the V-stretch, the wet jelq and the uli. The short version is that no published study has tested any of them by name, in healthy men, for the outcomes they are claimed to produce.

That sentence is doing a lot of work, so it is worth unpacking rather than leaving as a dismissal.

There is a real clinical literature on applying mechanical load to the penis. It concerns calibrated traction devices, worn for hours a day over months, in men with Peyronie's disease, and it is a genuine field with genuine findings. The practices on this page are a different thing: hand-applied, uncalibrated, undocumented in dose, and performed overwhelmingly by men who have no diagnosed condition. Borrowing the credibility of the device literature for a hand technique is the single most common error in this subject, and this page tries hard to avoid it.

What follows is what each method mechanically is, why men are drawn to it, what men report, what the literature establishes, what the risks are, and what is genuinely unknown.

1. What this page is and is not

It describes mechanics so you can recognize what is being discussed. It does not give a protocol: no repetitions, no durations, no pressures, no progressions, no schedules. If you came looking for how to perform these, that is deliberately absent.

It is also not a recommendation. Several of these methods have a plausible mechanism for causing harm and no demonstrated mechanism for producing the benefit claimed, and describing them accurately includes saying that.

One more thing to name up front, because the language around this subject obscures it. These techniques are traction and compression. Pulling the shaft under tension is traction. Gripping it to trap blood is compression. They are sometimes described as massage, which sounds gentler and is mechanically inaccurate, and this page uses the words that describe what is actually happening to the tissue.

2. How to read the labels

Four labels appear throughout. They are explained in full on the Research Lab page, and in short:

Supported by clinical evidence controlled human studies tested this and found an effect. Preliminary evidence published human research exists and is early. Community-reported, unverified men say so; nobody measured. Insufficient evidence the question is open and the literature cannot currently answer it.

A label is never the assessment. The prose next to it is.

3. The four methods

Directional pulls

Manual traction, applied along a chosen axis

What it generally involves

A grip is taken on the flaccid shaft and tension is applied away from the body, held, and directed along one axis at a time: straight out, downward, upward, or to one side. The mechanical event is tensile loading of the tunica albuginea and the surrounding structures. It is the hand-applied cousin of a traction device, with the differences that matter being calibration and duration: a device applies a known force for hours, a hand applies an unknown force for minutes.

Why men are interested

It is the technique with the most respectable looking neighbour. Penile traction therapy is a real clinical intervention, and the reasoning runs that if sustained tension helps in a clinical setting then hand-applied tension should do something similar. The appeal is also practical: it needs no equipment and nobody has to buy anything.

What men report

Community-reported, unverified

Reported positives include a sense of greater flaccid length, a looser or longer-hanging flaccid state, and a feeling of having done something constructive. Reported negatives include soreness, a dull ache lasting a day or two, temporary numbness in the glans, small red spots under the skin, and in some accounts a persistent loss of sensation or a change in curvature. None of these reports were measured, verified, or collected in a way that would show how often they occur.

What published research establishes

Insufficient evidence for the technique itself. No published trial has tested hand-applied directional pulls.

Supported by clinical evidence for the adjacent intervention, with important limits. Penile traction therapy using calibrated devices is an established area of study in Peyronie's disease, a condition involving fibrous plaque and curvature. Trial protocols in that field involve wearing a device for several hours a day over months. A systematic review of interventions in men complaining of small penis size found that extenders produced a small length gain, that vacuum devices produced none, and that the overall methodological quality of the literature was poor.

Those findings belong to devices, doses and populations that are not the ones on this page. Treating them as evidence for a hand technique in a healthy man is not a conservative reading of the literature; it is a different claim.

Risks

Documented for the general category of penile mechanical injury: haematoma, including septal haematoma, and in the worst case penile fracture, all of which appear in the peer-reviewed case report literature as consequences of force applied to the penis. Fibrosis following a bleed into the tissue is described in that literature.

Plausible for this technique specifically, from its mechanics: tensile loading of a structure that heals by scarring creates a route to exactly the plaque and curvature seen in Peyronie's disease. Healthy Male, the Australian government funded men's health service, makes this link explicitly for manual techniques. Nerve traction injury producing numbness is a mechanically plausible consequence of sustained pull on a structure carrying the dorsal nerve.

The distinction between documented and plausible is deliberate. We did not find a peer-reviewed case series on this named technique, and we are not going to imply one exists.

What remains unknown

Whether hand-applied tension reaches a force or duration capable of producing tissue adaptation at all. What force men actually apply, since nobody has measured it. Whether any reported flaccid change reflects tissue change or temporary stretch of skin and fascia. Whether the risk is meaningfully lower than for more aggressive methods or merely less often reported.

V-stretch

Manual traction with a fulcrum

What it generally involves

A directional pull with a pivot added. One hand pulls the shaft away from the body while a finger or the other hand presses at a point along the shaft, so the structure is bent around that point while under tension. The mechanical event is tensile loading combined with a concentrated bending moment at the fulcrum, which means the load is no longer spread along the shaft but focused where the pivot sits.

Why men are interested

The reasoning is that concentrating force should do more than spreading it, and that a stretch which feels more intense is doing more work. The sensation is noticeably stronger than a plain pull, which men tend to read as a sign of effectiveness.

What men report

Community-reported, unverified

Reported positives are the same family as directional pulls, with a stronger sensation of stretch. Reported negatives appear more often and more severely in accounts of this method than of plain pulls: localized pain at the fulcrum, bruising, and accounts of a new bend developing. The pattern is suggestive rather than evidential, because men who report it are self-selected and nobody is counting.

What published research establishes

Insufficient evidence

Nothing. No published study has evaluated this technique, and the adjacent device literature does not transfer, because a calibrated extender is specifically designed to distribute load along the shaft rather than concentrate it at a point. On the mechanics, the device evidence argues against this method rather than for it.

Risks

Documented for the category: as above, haematoma and fracture appear in the case report literature for mechanical force applied to the penis.

Plausible for this technique specifically: a concentrated bending moment on a tubular structure is the loading pattern most likely to produce a focal injury, and focal injury that heals with scar is the described route to Peyronie's-type plaque and curvature. Of the four methods on this page, this is the one whose mechanics most clearly point toward focal tissue damage.

What remains unknown

Everything about dose and threshold. Whether the fulcrum concentrates load enough to injure at the forces a hand can generate. Whether reported new curvature follows this method more often than others, which would need a register that does not exist.

Wet jelq

Lubricated compression stroke on a partially engorged shaft

What it generally involves

With lubricant and a partial erection, a ring grip is formed at the base with thumb and forefinger and drawn along the shaft toward the glans, repeatedly. The mechanical event is occlusive compression moving distally: the grip traps blood ahead of it and pushes it forward, raising pressure in the erectile tissue in front of the hand. Lubricant reduces friction on the skin. It does not reduce the pressure inside.

Why men are interested

It is the most widely discussed technique in this space and the one with the longest folk history, which gives it a credibility that has nothing to do with evidence. The claimed mechanism, that repeatedly forcing blood into the erectile tissue expands its capacity, is intuitive and easy to picture. It is also the technique most often described as a massage, which makes it sound benign.

What men report

Community-reported, unverified

Reported positives include a fuller flaccid appearance, a feeling of improved erection quality, and greater girth during the session. Reported negatives include bruising, broken capillaries visible as red dots, discoloration, loss of sensation, soreness, and accounts of worsened erection quality and new curvature. Reports of harm and reports of benefit come from the same forums and neither has been verified.

What published research establishes

Insufficient evidence is too generous a reading, so it is worth being precise: there is no evidence of benefit, and sources that have examined the practice are negative on it.

Healthy Male states there is no proof it works and that it can cause serious harm and scar tissue leading to painful and curved erections. Mayo Clinic's position on nonsurgical penis enlargement generally is that there is little scientific support, that most advertised techniques do not work, and that some can cause permanent damage. The systematic review of interventions in men complaining of small penis size found no support for this category and judged the overall literature to be of poor quality.

Risks

Documented for the category: haematoma and penile fracture in the case report literature. Mayo Clinic documents that overuse of vacuum devices, a different method sharing the feature of applied pressure, can damage elastic tissue and lead to less firm erections, which is directly relevant to anyone performing compression for the sake of erection quality.

Plausible for this technique specifically: repeated occlusive compression of vascular tissue is a mechanism for capillary rupture, which matches the bruising and red spotting men report, and for scarring where bleeding resolves. Healthy Male makes the scar tissue and Peyronie's link explicitly for this practice.

What remains unknown

Whether any of the reported fullness outlasts the session. Whether repeated pressure loading changes tissue capacity in any direction, including a worse one. What proportion of men who try it experience harm, since no denominator exists. Whether lubrication changes the risk profile at all, given that it addresses skin friction and not internal pressure.

Uli

Sustained compression hold on an erect or near-erect shaft

What it generally involves

A grip is closed around the base or mid-shaft and held while the penis is erect or close to it, so blood already in the erectile tissue is trapped distal to the grip and cannot drain. The mechanical event is sustained venous occlusion with the tissue under pressure. Unlike the jelq, the hand does not travel; the defining feature is duration of occlusion rather than repetition.

Why men are interested

It produces a dramatic and immediate visible effect, which is persuasive in a way that nothing on this page matches. The reasoning is that holding the tissue at maximum expansion should encourage it to accommodate more over time. It is also discussed as a way to experience a firmer erection in the moment, which shades into claims about erection quality.

What men report

Community-reported, unverified

Reported positives are almost entirely about the immediate visible effect and a sense of fullness afterward. Reported negatives are the most consistently alarming of the four: numbness, discoloration, prolonged dull ache, visible vessel damage, and accounts of lasting change in erection quality. Several community sources discourage the practice. That is still community opinion rather than evidence, and it is noted here because it is unusual for a community to argue against its own method.

What published research establishes

Insufficient evidence

No published study has evaluated it. The nearest relevant clinical knowledge is not about enlargement at all: prolonged obstruction of venous drainage from the erectile tissue is the mechanism underlying priapism, which is a urological emergency because trapped blood becomes deoxygenated and the tissue is damaged by prolonged ischaemia. No study has established at what duration deliberate occlusion becomes dangerous, which is a gap that should be read as a warning rather than as reassurance.

Risks

Documented for the category: ischaemic injury from prolonged obstruction of penile venous outflow is well established in the clinical literature on priapism, where the damage is a function of how long the tissue is deprived of oxygenated blood. Haematoma and fracture are documented for mechanical force generally.

Plausible for this technique specifically: deliberately recreating venous occlusion, by hand, for an uncontrolled duration, with no way to assess tissue oxygenation, is the mechanism of a known emergency applied intentionally. Nerve compression producing numbness follows directly from sustained circumferential pressure.

What remains unknown

At what duration and pressure meaningful harm begins. Whether the immediate visible effect has any relationship to lasting change. Whether reported lasting loss of erection quality is caused by the practice, since there is no study and no register.

4. The erection quality and blood flow claims

These deserve separate treatment, because they are the claims most often used to present manual techniques as a health practice rather than a size practice, and because they are the claims The Blueprint is most likely to be asked about.

Manual techniques improve erection quality
Insufficient evidence

No published study has tested any of these techniques against an erection quality measure. The claim rests on reported sensation during and shortly after a session, which is the period when the tissue is engorged from the handling itself. Mayo Clinic's documented finding that overuse of pressure-based devices can lead to less firm erections points in the opposite direction, and is the nearest thing to direct evidence on the question.

They increase blood flow to the penis in a lasting way
Insufficient evidence

Moving blood into a region by squeezing it there is not the same as improving the circulation that supplies it. An erection depends on arteries that widen on demand and nerves that carry the signal to widen them, which is what NIDDK describes when it lists the causes of erectile difficulty. Nothing in these techniques has been shown to act on arterial function or nerve signaling, and no study has measured penile blood flow before and after them.

They produce permanent growth in length or girth
Insufficient evidence

No study supports it for these techniques. In the adjacent device literature, a systematic review found extenders produced a small length gain and vacuum devices produced none, in a body of work the reviewers judged to be of poor methodological quality. Mayo Clinic states there is little scientific support for nonsurgical enlargement. Healthy Male states there is no proof jelqing works.

They are safe as long as you are gentle
Insufficient evidence

This one is worth naming as the most dangerous claim on the page, because it sounds like caution. No study has established a safe force, a safe duration, or a threshold below which these techniques do not cause harm. Nobody has measured what force men apply when they believe they are being gentle. A threshold nobody has measured cannot be a safety margin, and describing compression and traction as massage does not change what the tissue experiences.

5. What would change the picture

It is worth being concrete about what is missing, because the absence of evidence here is not a permanent condition.

  • A controlled trial of any one of these techniques, by name, in healthy men, with a defined dose and a real control group.
  • Measurement of the forces and pressures men actually apply, which would at least allow the adjacent device literature to be compared meaningfully.
  • Before and after penile haemodynamic measurement, which would settle the blood flow claim directly.
  • A prospective register of adverse events with a known denominator, so that the rate of harm could be stated rather than guessed.
  • Longer follow-up than any existing study of mechanical intervention in healthy men provides.

Until those exist, anybody who tells you these techniques work, or that they are safe, is telling you something the literature does not currently support in either direction.

6. When to see a doctor

Regardless of anything on this page, see a clinician promptly if you have:

  • An erection lasting more than four hours, which is a urological emergency.
  • Pain, bruising, swelling or discoloration of the penis.
  • A new bend, lump or area of hardness.
  • Numbness or loss of sensation.
  • Blood in your urine or semen.
  • Difficulty passing urine.
  • A sudden change in erection quality.

If what prompted your interest is worry about size, both the NHS and Healthy Male note that concern is common and the size itself usually is not the problem. The systematic review cited below found that counseling helped many men complaining of small penis size, which is a finding most of the physical interventions in that review could not match.
